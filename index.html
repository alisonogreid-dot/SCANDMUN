Worked Example: Republic of Nicaragua

This example shows you how to research a country and, more importantly, why each piece of information matters for the two GA topics. Use this as a model when preparing your own country.

🇳🇮

Republic of Nicaragua

Central America • Population ~7 million • Capital: Managua

Geography & Culture

Official nameRepublic of Nicaragua
RegionCentral America, bordered by Honduras and Costa Rica
Population~7 million
ReligionPredominantly Roman Catholic (~47%), with growing Protestant/Evangelical population (~33%)
LanguagesSpanish (official), Indigenous languages on the Caribbean coast (Miskito, Sumo)
HDI rankingLow-to-medium human development; one of the poorest countries in the Western Hemisphere
💡 Why does this matter? Nicaragua’s strong Catholic identity is central to understanding its abortion policy. The research report notes that the Christian church views abortion as a grave moral sin, and in Nicaragua, the Catholic Church is a powerful political actor. The 2006 total abortion ban was passed shortly before elections, widely understood as part of a political deal between President Ortega and the Church. When writing your country report, always ask: “How does religion or culture shape my country’s stance on the GA topics?”

Politics & Government

Government typePresidential republic (authoritarian tendencies under current government)
Current leaderPresident Daniel Ortega (Sandinista National Liberation Front / FSLN)
Key alliancesALBA bloc (Venezuela, Cuba), generally aligned with Russia and China in UN voting
Relations with the WestStrained; subject to US and EU sanctions over human rights concerns
History of conflictSandinista Revolution (1979), Contra War (1981–1990) — Cold War proxy conflict
💡 Why does this matter? Government type tells you how decisions are made and how likely your country is to change policy. Nicaragua’s centralised, authoritarian governance means that policy on abortion and child protection reflects the president’s priorities, not a free legislative debate. It also means Nicaragua is unlikely to accept international “interference” in domestic policy — delegates should expect to emphasise national sovereignty. For child soldiers, the Contra War is critical: both sides used child combatants, making Nicaragua a country with direct historical experience of this issue.

Economy

GDP per capita~$2,300 (one of the lowest in Latin America)
Key sectorsAgriculture (coffee, tobacco, sugar), textiles, tourism
Poverty rate~25% living below the poverty line, higher in rural areas
Healthcare spendingLow; limited rural healthcare infrastructure
💡 Why does this matter? Economic data helps you understand what your country can realistically commit to. Nicaragua cannot fund large-scale reproductive health programmes without international support, which shapes what kind of clauses your delegate would support or oppose. For child soldiers, poverty is identified in the research report as a primary push factor — Nicaragua’s high poverty rate and history of conflict explain why children were historically vulnerable to recruitment. When drafting clauses, consider: “Does my country need financial assistance to implement this?”

Connecting Country Facts to the Topics

This is the most important step: drawing lines between what you know about your country and the specific GA topics. Here’s how each fact about Nicaragua connects:

Topic 1: Abortion

Total ban since 2006. Nicaragua criminalises all abortion without exception — even when the mother’s life is at risk or the pregnancy results from rape. Women face up to 2 years in prison; doctors face up to 6 years. This is one of the most restrictive policies in the world.

Topic 2: Child Soldiers

Historical use during the Contra War. Both the Sandinista government and US-backed Contra rebels used child soldiers in the 1980s conflict. Nicaragua has since ratified the CRC and acceded to the OPAC in 2005, setting 18 as the minimum military recruitment age.

Topic 1: International pressure

Repeated UN criticism. CEDAW and the Human Rights Committee have found Nicaragua’s total ban violates international human rights law. In January 2025, the HRC ruled in two cases (Lucía and Susana) that Nicaragua’s absolute criminalisation was incompatible with human rights obligations.

Topic 2: Current status

No current child soldier problem. Nicaragua’s armed forces report no under-18 recruits today. However, Nicaragua’s experience gives it a credible voice on rehabilitation and reintegration — and on why poverty reduction is essential to prevention.

Topic 1: The “why” behind the ban

Religion + political power. The ban was a political deal between President Ortega and the Catholic Church before the 2006 election. This shows how the research report’s point about religion affecting policy plays out in practice — even when polling shows ~69% of Nicaraguans supported therapeutic abortion.

Topic 2: Root causes

Poverty and instability. The research report identifies poverty, political instability, and lack of education as key push factors. Nicaragua experienced all three during the Contra War era, which is why children were drawn into the conflict on both sides.

Treaty Ratification Status

Always check which treaties your country has ratified — this tells you what they have already committed to and what you can hold them to in debate.

CEDAW✅ Ratified (1981) — but the abortion ban contradicts CEDAW Committee recommendations
CRC✅ Ratified (1990) — committed to protecting children’s rights to safety and education
CRC-OPAC✅ Acceded (2005) — declared minimum military recruitment age of 18
Rome Statute (ICC)❌ Not ratified — limits international accountability mechanisms
💡 Why does this matter? Nicaragua has ratified CEDAW but maintains a total abortion ban — this is a contradiction that other delegates can challenge in debate. As the Nicaraguan delegate, you would need to argue that national sovereignty allows the state to interpret CEDAW within its own cultural and legal framework. On child soldiers, Nicaragua’s ratification of both the CRC and OPAC means it has strong legal commitments to support — use this to argue for strengthening prevention programmes.

What Would a Nicaraguan Delegate Argue?

On Abortion

Nicaragua would likely resist any resolution that frames abortion access as a human right. The delegate might argue for national sovereignty over reproductive policy, emphasise the right to life from conception, and redirect the conversation toward maternal healthcare and reducing poverty as alternatives. However, the delegate would be under pressure given repeated UN findings against Nicaragua’s total ban.

On Child Soldiers

Nicaragua would likely support strong measures against child recruitment, citing its own experience during the Contra War and its ratification of the CRC and OPAC. The delegate could advocate for poverty reduction and education as prevention strategies, and call for rehabilitation programmes funded by the international community — aligning with its need for development assistance.

🎯 Key lesson: Notice how the same country can be conservative on one topic and progressive on another. Your research should help you understand the reasons behind each position — religion, history, economics, political alliances — not just what the position is. This is what makes a strong MUN delegate: understanding the why.
